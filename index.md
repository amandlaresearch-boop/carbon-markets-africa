# Carbon Markets in Africa: A Practical Reference

Carbon markets have moved from a specialist niche to a working part of how businesses, governments, and investors approach emissions. Africa is increasingly at the center of that shift. This page is a plain-language reference for anyone trying to understand how these markets actually work, what the key terms mean, and where the African regulatory landscape now stands.

It is maintained as a free educational resource. If you spot something that has changed or could be clearer, contributions and corrections are welcome.

## The two types of carbon market

Almost every conversation about carbon markets comes back to a single distinction.

**Compliance markets** exist because a law or regulation forces certain emitters to limit what they release. Governments cap total emissions, issue or auction allowances, and require covered companies to hold enough allowances to match their output. The best known example is the European Union Emissions Trading System. Aviation has its own scheme under CORSIA. Participation is not optional for the entities these rules cover.

**Voluntary markets** exist because organizations choose to act, not because they are compelled to. A company aiming for a net zero target might buy carbon credits to offset emissions it cannot yet eliminate. Here the demand comes from corporate commitments, brand values, and stakeholder pressure rather than statute. This is the market most African project developers engage with first.

The two are converging. As compliance schemes expand and as Article 6 of the Paris Agreement opens formal channels between countries, the line between voluntary action and regulated trade is getting thinner.

## Core concepts, in plain terms

**Carbon credit.** A tradable certificate representing one tonne of carbon dioxide equivalent that has been reduced, avoided, or removed from the atmosphere. Buying and retiring a credit is how an organization claims that reduction against its own footprint.

**Offset.** The act of compensating for your own emissions by paying for an equivalent reduction somewhere else. A credit is the instrument; offsetting is the use you put it to.

**Baseline.** The emissions that would have happened without the project. Everything a project claims is measured against this counterfactual, which is why baselines are scrutinized so heavily.

**Additionality.** The test of whether a project's reductions are genuinely extra. If the activity would have happened anyway, without carbon finance, it is not additional and should not generate credits. This is one of the most contested questions in the whole field.

**Permanence.** Whether a reduction lasts. A forest that stores carbon for thirty years and then burns raises hard questions about whether its credits were ever real. Strong projects plan for permanence and set aside buffers against reversal.

**Leakage.** When protecting or changing one area simply pushes the emitting activity somewhere else. Credible accounting has to account for it rather than ignore it.

**MRV.** Monitoring, reporting, and verification. The backbone of any credible credit. It is the system that measures what a project actually achieved, documents it, and has it checked by an independent third party.

**Validation and verification.** Validation confirms, before a project starts, that its design is sound. Verification confirms, after the fact, that the claimed reductions really occurred. Both are carried out by accredited independent auditors.

**Retirement.** The permanent cancellation of a credit in a registry so it can never be sold or claimed again. A credit only does its job once it is retired.

## The main standards

Credits are only as trustworthy as the standard behind them. A few dominate the voluntary market.

**Verified Carbon Standard (VCS), run by Verra,** is the most widely used voluntary standard by volume. It sets the rules and methodologies that projects follow and operates a registry that tracks credits from issuance to retirement.

**Gold Standard** places particular emphasis on sustainable development benefits alongside emission reductions, and is often favored where community and social outcomes matter as much as the tonnes.

Both are worth understanding because buyers increasingly ask not just how many credits a project produces, but which standard certifies them and how rigorous its methodology is.

## The African and Kenyan landscape

Africa holds a large share of the world's untapped, high-quality reduction potential, particularly in nature-based and community-based projects. What has changed recently is the regulation around it.

Kenya has moved faster than most. A short timeline:

- The **Climate Change Act of 2016** first acknowledged carbon markets.
- The **Climate Change (Amendment) Act of 2023** gave carbon markets a real legal foundation and set out participation rules.
- The **Climate Change (Carbon Markets) Regulations of 2024** established how projects are reviewed, approved, and governed, and placed the Designated National Authority function within the National Environment Management Authority.
- The **Climate Change (Carbon Trading) Regulations of 2025** defined how credits are traded and how proceeds are handled.
- In **February 2026, the Kenya National Carbon Registry went live**, hosted at the National Environment Management Authority in Nairobi. It serves as the country's central platform for tracking projects, verifying reductions, and authorizing transfers, including internationally transferred mitigation outcomes under Article 6 of the Paris Agreement.

One provision stands out and signals where the continent is heading. Under Kenya's carbon trading rules, a defined share of carbon credit proceeds, set at 25 percent, must flow back to local community projects. This responds directly to years of criticism that carbon revenue bypassed the communities hosting the underlying projects. Expect community consent, benefit-sharing, and documented local participation to become baseline requirements that serious buyers insist on, not optional extras.

The practical takeaway for professionals: the market is being formalized in real time, and the skills to develop projects to standard, document them credibly, and navigate the new rules are in short supply relative to the opportunity.

## A short glossary of acronyms

- **CORSIA** — Carbon Offsetting and Reduction Scheme for International Aviation
- **EU ETS** — European Union Emissions Trading System
- **ITMO** — Internationally Transferred Mitigation Outcome
- **MRV** — Monitoring, Reporting, and Verification
- **NDC** — Nationally Determined Contribution, a country's climate pledge under the Paris Agreement
- **REDD+** — Reducing Emissions from Deforestation and Forest Degradation, plus conservation and sustainable management
- **VCM** — Voluntary Carbon Market
- **VCS** — Verified Carbon Standard
